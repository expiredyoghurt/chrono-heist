# Chrono Heist — Full Case Walkthrough

This document lays out the story, every branching choice, and the outcome of
every path for all 10 civilizations in Chrono Heist. Each case is a two-step
branching decision tree: your first choice (A/B/C) leads to a follow-up
decision, which leads to one of 9 possible endings ("leaves").

**Outcome tiers, used throughout:**

| Tier | Meaning | Points | Artifact recovered? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Ideal** | The best possible read of the evidence — case fully cracked | +30 | Yes |
| 🟡 **Non-Ideal (Passable)** | A workable but incomplete resolution | +12 | No |
| ⚪ **Non-Ideal (Trail Went Cold)** | A dead end — no real harm done, just no progress | 0 | No |
| 🔴 **Catastrophic (Case Mishandled)** | An active misstep that burns the lead or damages trust | −10 | No |

## 1. 🪔 The Silent Seals of Mohenjo-daro
**Location:** Mohenjo-daro, Indus Valley  |  **Era:** c. 2500 BCE

### Briefing
You materialize on a sun-baked street in Mohenjo-daro, one of the great cities of the Indus Valley, laid out in the same precise grid pattern the Agency briefed you on. Word has already reached the local ward-keeper: a set of carved steatite seals, prized for their finely etched unicorn motifs and undeciphered script, have vanished from a merchant's house beside the Great Bath, the city's famous public bathing structure. In the empty display niche, someone has left behind a single seal cut from a dull grey metal that has no business existing in this century.

The merchant's neighbors are just as unsettled as you are. Down the lane, the riverside marketplace is still buzzing with traders from as far as Gujarat and the Persian Gulf, any one of whom might have slipped through unnoticed during the crowded morning trade. Above the rooftops looms the citadel mound, where the city's granary stands — the same raised platform where administrators track incoming grain shipments using seals almost identical to the ones just stolen.

Your Agency briefing flags three leads worth chasing immediately: the bathhouse and merchant's house themselves, the marketplace crowd, and the citadel granary's own seal records. Somewhere among fired bricks, drainage channels, and unicorn seals, the Anachronist has left a trail — if you know how this city's obsessively standardized world actually works.

### Decision Tree

**Opening question:** Where do you begin your investigation?
> *Stakes hint: The building method itself is the giveaway here, if you know where to look for it.*

- **A.** Examine the Great Bath and the merchant's house for construction and drainage clues
- **B.** Interview traders in the riverside marketplace, since gossip about unfamiliar faces spreads fast among stallkeepers
- **C.** Check the citadel granary's seal-based grain records, since administrators there track every shipment with obsessive precision

#### Path A: "Examine the Great Bath and the merchant's house for construction and drainage clues"

**Follow-up question:** The dust you find at the scene doesn't match any local kiln. How do you follow up?

- **AA.** Compare the brick's dimensions to the standardized ratio used across the whole civilization
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** You match the odd brick dust to no kiln on Earth — a residue that couldn't exist for another four thousand years. Following the standardized-brick logic the Indus builders used across their entire civilization, you trace the thief's escape route past the drainage channels and up onto the citadel mound, recovering the stolen seals from a hidden compartment inside the granary itself.
- **AB.** Ask a local artisan to identify the unfamiliar material, since craftsmen here pride themselves on knowing every stone and clay by feel
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** The artisan can't identify the strange material, but he does recall an unfamiliar trader haggling near the merchant's house days earlier. It's not enough for an arrest, but the Agency logs a strong new suspect profile, and you recover a partial wax impression of one missing seal.
- **AC.** Assume the dust points to a nearby trader and publicly accuse him on the spot, before anyone can verify the claim
  - **Outcome (🔴 CATASTROPHIC (Case Mishandled), -10 pts):** Acting on a hunch, you publicly accuse a well-known local trader in the marketplace — and you're wrong. The real thief, watching from the crowd, realizes an agent is on the case and disappears for good, taking the seals with them. The Agency's report is blunt: certainty without evidence is its own kind of failure.

#### Path B: "Interview traders in the riverside marketplace, since gossip about unfamiliar faces spreads fast among stallkeepers"

**Follow-up question:** A trader claims he saw a figure vanish near the citadel mound at dusk. How do you handle this lead?

- **BA.** Cross-check his story against the timing of the citadel's well-regulated street activity
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** Cross-checking the witness's timeline against the city's own carefully managed street activity near the citadel, the story holds up perfectly. You follow the same route the figure used, through the drainage channels and up onto the mound, and recover the stolen seals from a hidden compartment inside the granary.
- **BB.** Trust him immediately and rush to the citadel alone, certain speed matters more than a plan right now
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You rush to the citadel alone and arrive breathless — and empty-handed. Whoever the trader saw is long gone, and without backup or a plan, you've lost the trail. The seals are never recovered.
- **BC.** Dismiss him as unreliable and return to the marketplace crowd, where a firmer lead seems more likely to turn up
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You write the trader off as unreliable and head back to the marketplace, but the crowd has thinned and the moment has passed. Without that lead, the trail goes cold.

#### Path C: "Check the citadel granary's seal-based grain records, since administrators there track every shipment with obsessive precision"

**Follow-up question:** The granary's grain ledgers are extensive and confusing. How do you narrow the search?

- **CA.** Focus on entries stamped in the same unusual style as the stolen seals
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** Cross-referencing entries stamped in the same distinctive style as the stolen seals, you narrow the record to a handful of suspicious transactions. It's not a full recovery, but the pattern gives the Agency's archivists enough to reconstruct much of what happened.
- **CB.** Question the granary administrator directly about who's had access
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** The granary administrator is cooperative, and together you identify irregular access on the night of the theft. The seals themselves are never found, but the administrator's account fills crucial gaps in the Agency's case file.
- **CC.** Assume the thief wouldn't risk the heavily monitored granary and abandon this lead
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You assume the thief would never risk the heavily monitored granary and abandon the lead — a mistake, since that's exactly where the trail led. The seals are never recovered.

### The Real History
The Indus Valley Civilisation (also called the Harappan Civilisation) flourished across what is now Pakistan and northwest India from roughly 3300–1300 BCE, making it a contemporary of early Mesopotamia and Egypt. Its cities, including Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, are remarkable for extraordinarily advanced urban planning: grid-patterned streets, standardized fired-brick sizes used across hundreds of kilometers, and the world's earliest known urban sanitation systems, with covered drains running beneath nearly every street. The civilization also produced thousands of carved seals bearing an undeciphered script — one of the great unsolved puzzles of archaeology, since no "Rosetta Stone" for Indus script has ever been found.

Modern successor: the standardized, sanitation-first city planning pioneered here anticipates modern municipal engineering — today's building codes, zoning grids, and underground sewer systems trace a conceptual line back to Mohenjo-daro's drains. South Asia's contemporary emphasis on standardized weights and measures in trade also echoes the uniform brick ratios and weight-stones Harappan merchants used to trade fairly across their vast trade network.

**Recovered Artifact (on Ideal path):** 🪔 Steatite Merchant Seal — A carved seal bearing undeciphered Indus script.

## 2. 𓋹 The Displaced Cartouche
**Location:** Giza, Old Kingdom Egypt  |  **Era:** c. 2560 BCE

### Briefing
You arrive at Giza in the shadow of the half-built pyramid complex, dust and noise everywhere as thousands of laborers haul limestone under the Old Kingdom sun. Word spreads fast: a carved cartouche relief bearing a pharaoh's name has been pried clean off a tomb wall overnight. In the gap left behind, workers found a strange lightweight sheet unlike any stone, wood, or plaster used anywhere in Egypt.

The pyramid site is a small city in itself. Scribes keep a record office nearby, tracking which crew of stonemasons worked which section each day; the crews themselves are still hard at work on the current shift, chiseling and hauling blocks into place. Beyond the site, the desert perimeter stretches toward the workers' villages, and further off, past the mortuary temple, sit the embalming workshops where priests prepare bodies for the afterlife.

Any of these places could hold the thief's trail — the meticulous scribal records, the stonemasons who had hands-on access to the tomb wall, or the open desert where a fleeing figure might leave footprints. The Agency reminds you: Old Kingdom Egypt ran on careful bookkeeping, so the answer is probably written down somewhere.

### Decision Tree

**Opening question:** How do you begin investigating the missing cartouche relief?
> *Stakes hint: Every path leads somewhere — but only one gets you to the relief before it's lost for good.*

- **A.** Study the tomb's construction records kept by the scribes overseeing the pyramid workforce
- **B.** Question the stonemasons currently on shift, since they were the last people confirmed near the tomb wall
- **C.** Search the desert perimeter for footprints, reasoning a fleeing thief would have to cross open sand to escape

#### Path A: "Study the tomb's construction records kept by the scribes overseeing the pyramid workforce"

**Follow-up question:** The scribe records mention a "foreman's mark" system used to track which crew worked where. How do you use it?

- **AA.** Cross-reference the mark left at the crime scene against the foreman logs to identify which crew had access that night
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** Cross-referencing the crime-scene mark against the foreman logs — a real Old Kingdom method for tracking thousands of workers by crew — turns up a work gang that shouldn't have existed that day. You corner them near the embalming workshops and recover the cartouche relief intact, moments before it would have been lost to the sands forever.
- **AB.** Ask a supervisor for his best guess instead of digging through the records yourself, trusting his years of experience on site
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** The supervisor's guess points you toward a plausible crew, but without the actual cross-reference you can't be certain, and by the time you narrow it down further the relief has already changed hands. Still, your report documents the foreman-mark system precisely enough for historians to understand exactly how the crime happened.
- **AC.** Publicly accuse the crew whose mark looks closest, without finishing the cross-reference, since acting fast feels more urgent than being thorough
  - **Outcome (🔴 CATASTROPHIC (Case Mishandled), -10 pts):** You accuse a crew based on a mark that only looks similar, not confirmed. The real crew's foreman, embarrassed and furious, refuses to cooperate further, and word spreads across the site that accusations are being made without proof — burying whatever thin trail remained.

#### Path B: "Question the stonemasons currently on shift, since they were the last people confirmed near the tomb wall"

**Follow-up question:** One stonemason grows nervous and mentions seeing someone unfamiliar near the tomb wall at dawn. How do you follow up?

- **BA.** Cross-check his account against the foreman's own crew-assignment records for that shift
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** The stonemason's account checks out perfectly against the crew-assignment records, confirming exactly who had access to the tomb wall that morning. You catch the crew mid-attempt to smuggle the relief out through the workers' village and recover it intact.
- **BB.** Trust his word completely and rush straight for the desert perimeter to search for footprints
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You take the stonemason's word without confirming it and rush into the open desert, but the footprints there turn out to belong to an unrelated laborer. By the time you double back, the relief is long gone.
- **BC.** Dismiss him as unreliable, since nervous witnesses are common on a site this large
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You write the mason off as an unreliable witness on a busy site, but it turns out he was the only person who actually saw the thief. The case goes cold.

#### Path C: "Search the desert perimeter for footprints, reasoning a fleeing thief would have to cross open sand to escape"

**Follow-up question:** The footprints lead toward the embalming workshops before vanishing on rockier ground. What now?

- **CA.** Follow the embalming-ritual angle, using funerary text knowledge to bluff a suspect into revealing his motive
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** Bluffing your way through a conversation about funerary ritual, you get a suspect talking — he lets slip enough about the theft's motive for the Agency to close out most of the case file, even though the relief itself is never physically recovered.
- **CB.** Threaten the first embalming worker you find, betting fear will make someone talk fast
  - **Outcome (🔴 CATASTROPHIC (Case Mishandled), -10 pts):** Threatening the first worker you find backfires badly — he's entirely uninvolved, and the embalming workshop staff, offended by the accusation, refuse to speak with Agency investigators again for the rest of the case.
- **CC.** Give up on the trail since the footprints disappeared, and report back empty-handed
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You call off the search once the footprints vanish on rocky ground. The trail was recoverable with a bit more patience — but you'll never know, because the case goes cold here.

### The Real History
Egypt's Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BCE) is often called the "Age of the Pyramids," producing the Step Pyramid of Djoser and the Great Pyramid of Giza, built under Pharaoh Khufu. Far from slave-built myths, evidence from workers' villages shows the pyramids were constructed by organized, paid labor crews rotated on a schedule, supported by state-run bakeries and breweries. Egyptian scribes maintained meticulous administrative records, and elaborate funerary texts and rituals reflected a civilization deeply organized around the idea of an ordered afterlife mirroring the ordered state.

Modern successor: the Old Kingdom's bureaucratic record-keeping — assigning workers to numbered crews, tracking rations and shifts — is an early ancestor of modern project management and payroll systems. Egyptology itself remains a living field, and modern Egypt's tourism economy and museum system (like the Grand Egyptian Museum) are direct stewards of this legacy today.

**Recovered Artifact (on Ideal path):** 𓋹 Limestone Cartouche Relief — A carved royal name-plate from a Giza tomb wall.

## 3. 📜 The Missing Pillar of Law
**Location:** Babylon, Mesopotamia  |  **Era:** c. 1750 BCE

### Briefing
You step into the mudbrick sprawl of Babylon, where the great ziggurat Etemenanki rises above the rooftops as part of the temple complex devoted to Marduk, the city's patron god. Priests are in an uproar: the black diorite stela inscribed with the king's law code has vanished from the temple grounds. Witnesses describe a flash of light and a strange humming sound in the moments before it disappeared, leaving no obvious trace.

The temple itself keeps a vast archive of clay tablets, recording court judgments, land grants, and trade disputes settled under the very laws now missing. Priests move constantly between the ziggurat's shrines and the archive rooms, while just beyond the temple walls, the Euphrates riverbank bustles with boats unloading grain and timber. Further out, a web of irrigation canals — engineered with famous Babylonian precision — laces the farmland surrounding the city.

Any of these threads might lead you to the stela: the temple's own records, the priests who guard them, or the riverside routes a thief could use to vanish. The Agency suspects whoever took it needed to understand this city's laws well enough to know exactly what they were destroying.

### Decision Tree

**Opening question:** Where do you begin the search for the missing law stela?
> *Stakes hint: The right lead traces all the way to the thief. The wrong one just documents the loss.*

- **A.** Examine the temple's clay-tablet archive for records of who last accessed the stela
- **B.** Question the temple priests moving between the shrines and the archive throughout the day
- **C.** Search the riverbank for signs of the thief's escape route leading away from the city

#### Path A: "Examine the temple's clay-tablet archive for records of who last accessed the stela"

**Follow-up question:** The tablets reveal a recent merchant dispute judged using the very laws now missing. How do you follow it?

- **AA.** Use the code's own proportional-justice logic to work out who felt wronged enough to want the law itself erased
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** The code's own logic of proportional justice points you straight at a merchant who felt the ruling against him was excessive — and who had both the grudge and the temple access to take the stela himself. You catch him just as he's preparing to destroy it for good, and recover all 282 laws intact.
- **AB.** Ask the merchant directly with no preparation, hoping he simply volunteers the truth once confronted
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** Caught off guard, the merchant denies everything but stumbles over details only someone close to the theft would know. It's not a confession, but the Agency logs enough to identify him as the likely culprit, even without recovering the stela itself.
- **AC.** Assume the dispute is unrelated and abandon this thread entirely, since a business complaint seems too mundane to matter here
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** Assuming the dispute is unrelated costs you the one thread that actually led somewhere. Without it, the trail dries up entirely.

#### Path B: "Question the temple priests moving between the shrines and the archive throughout the day"

**Follow-up question:** A priest reluctantly admits to seeing an unfamiliar figure near the ziggurat archive at dusk. How do you handle it?

- **BA.** Cross-check the priest's account against temple access logs for that hour
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** Checking the priest's account against the temple's access logs confirms his story down to the hour, and reveals the intruder used a side entrance normally reserved for junior clergy. You follow the same route to a hidden storeroom and recover the stela before real damage is done.
- **BB.** Confront the priest publicly in front of the temple staff, demanding he explain why he waited to mention it
  - **Outcome (🔴 CATASTROPHIC (Case Mishandled), -10 pts):** Publicly pressed in front of his colleagues, the priest grows defensive and stops cooperating altogether — and worse, the temple hierarchy grows wary of Agency involvement, making every remaining lead inside the temple far harder to pursue.
- **BC.** Dismiss the account, since priests are famously protective of the temple's reputation
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You dismiss the priest's account as temple politics, but it turns out to be the most solid lead in the entire case. By the time you realize your mistake, too much time has passed.

#### Path C: "Search the riverbank for signs of the thief's escape route leading away from the city"

**Follow-up question:** You find a scorched patch of earth near the riverbank. What do you make of it?

- **CA.** Compare it against the irrigation canal maps to see who could reach this remote spot undetected
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** Cross-referencing the scorched patch against Babylon's famously precise irrigation canal maps narrows the escape route to a single unguarded lock. You catch the thief mid-escape downstream, and the stela — with its 282 laws, among the oldest detailed legal codes on Earth — is safely returned to the temple.
- **CB.** Assume it's a lightning strike and drop the lead entirely, since scorched ground near open plains isn't unusual
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You write the scorched earth off as a lightning strike and move on. It wasn't — and the trail goes cold as a result.
- **CC.** Report it without further analysis and wait for someone else back at the temple to interpret what it means
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** You report the scorched patch without analyzing it yourself, and by the time anyone else gets around to it, the thief has long since used the canal network to disappear downstream.

### The Real History
Ancient Mesopotamia, the land "between two rivers" (Tigris and Euphrates), gave rise to some of humanity's first cities, writing systems (cuneiform), and codified laws. The Code of Hammurabi, carved around 1754 BCE, is one of the earliest and most complete written legal codes, covering everything from trade disputes to family law, organized around the principle of proportional justice. Mesopotamians also pioneered large-scale irrigation canals that turned an arid floodplain into some of the most productive farmland in the ancient world, and their base-60 number system still shapes how we measure time.

Modern successor: written, codified law — the idea that rules should be public, consistent, and apply to disputes predictably — underlies virtually every modern legal system. And every time you check a clock with 60 minutes or a circle with 360 degrees, you're using Mesopotamian mathematics.

## 4. 🏛️ The Stolen Assembly Scroll
**Location:** Athens, Classical Greece  |  **Era:** c. 450 BCE

### Briefing
You arrive on the Pnyx, the rocky hill where the Athenian Assembly gathers to debate and vote on the affairs of the city — the beating heart of the world's first democracy. A vital scroll recording the Assembly's procedures has gone missing just before a major vote, and the presiding officials are in a panic. A citizen no one recognizes was seen nearby, wearing clothing "from no polis anyone knew."

Beside the speaker's platform sits the klepsydra, a water-clock used to time each citizen's turn to speak, its steady drip a fixture of every session. Down the hill, the agora marketplace hums with vendors, gossip, and citizens waiting to hear the day's news. Nearby, a knot of men debate philosophy loudly — Athens being a city where sortition, the random lottery system used to select jurors and many officials, is as familiar a topic as the weather.

One elderly philosopher watches the commotion with obvious interest, occasionally offering unsolicited opinions to anyone who'll listen. Between the water-clock's records, the crowded agora, and this philosopher's suspicious attentiveness, the Agency is confident the scroll hasn't gone far — democracy, after all, rarely keeps secrets for long.

### Decision Tree

**Opening question:** How do you begin your investigation on the Pnyx?
> *Stakes hint: One thread leads to the philosopher watching from the crowd. The others just watch time run out.*

- **A.** Check the klepsydra records used to time speakers, to establish exactly when the scroll vanished
- **B.** Ask citizens gathered on the Pnyx what they noticed in the confusion right after the scroll disappeared
- **C.** Search the agora marketplace, on the theory a thief would try to sell the scroll quickly

#### Path A: "Check the klepsydra records used to time speakers, to establish exactly when the scroll vanished"

**Follow-up question:** The klepsydra records show the scroll vanished during an unusually long gap in speaking turns. What do you make of it?

- **AA.** Cross-reference that gap against the sortition records to see who was suspiciously absent from jury selection that same morning
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** Cross-referencing the timing gap against the sortition records turns up exactly one citizen missing from the jury pool that morning — the elderly philosopher who's been hovering near the investigation ever since. Your Socratic cross-examination trips him into contradicting his own alibi, and you recover the Assembly scroll just as the vote is about to be postponed indefinitely.
- **AB.** Ask a random citizen nearby if they noticed anything unusual during that unusually long gap between speeches that morning
  - **Outcome (🟡 NON-IDEAL (Passable), +12 pts):** The citizen you ask doesn't remember much, but one detail — an unfamiliar face near the speaker's platform — narrows your suspect list considerably. The scroll itself is never physically recovered, but the Agency's account of the theft is solid enough to close most of the file.
- **AC.** Assume the timing is a coincidence and move on without checking it further, since long gaps aren't unheard of during a full day of debate
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** Assuming the timing is coincidence, you let the strongest lead in the case slip past you. The scroll is never found.

#### Path B: "Ask citizens gathered on the Pnyx what they noticed in the confusion right after the scroll disappeared"

**Follow-up question:** A citizen mentions the thief avoided being chosen by lot for jury duty that morning. What does that suggest?

- **BA.** Use the sortition records to see exactly who was missing from the pool entirely
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** The sortition records confirm it: the elderly philosopher, of all people, was never registered in the citizen rolls to begin with — a serious irregularity in a city built on public accountability. Confronted with the proof, he cracks, and you recover the Assembly scroll before the vote can be postponed.
- **BB.** Confront the nearest citizen you suspect directly, based on the tip alone, before anyone can verify it further
  - **Outcome (🔴 CATASTROPHIC (Case Mishandled), -10 pts):** Confronting a citizen based on a tip alone, with no evidence, humiliates an innocent man in front of the Assembly — and word spreads fast in a democracy this tightly knit. Whatever cooperation might have followed dries up completely.
- **BC.** Ignore it, since avoiding jury duty seems like a minor civic complaint unrelated to a stolen scroll
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** Jury-duty avoidance seems like a strange detail to chase, so you drop it. It turns out to be the one loose thread that would have unraveled the whole case.

#### Path C: "Search the agora marketplace, on the theory a thief would try to sell the scroll quickly"

**Follow-up question:** In the agora, an elderly philosopher offers to help — for a price. Do you accept?

- **CA.** Decline his offer and cross-examine him using the Socratic method to see if his answers contradict themselves
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** Your Socratic cross-examination trips the philosopher into contradicting his own alibi — he was never registered in the citizen rolls to begin with. You recover the Assembly scroll just as the vote is about to be postponed indefinitely.
- **CB.** Pay him and hope for the best, trusting that a coin or two will loosen his tongue faster than questions would
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** Paying him buys you nothing but vague, evasive answers that lead nowhere. The scroll is gone, and so is your coin.
- **CC.** Refuse and walk away entirely, deciding a philosopher this eager to talk for money is more trouble than he's worth
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** Walking away costs you the only lead willing to talk to you at all. The philosopher's answers, frustrating as they might have been, were closer to the truth than you'll ever know.

### The Real History
Classical Athens (5th century BCE) is considered the birthplace of democracy. Under leaders like Pericles, adult male citizens could speak and vote directly in the Assembly on the Pnyx hill, while juries and many offices were filled by sortition — random lottery — rather than election, to prevent wealthy citizens from buying influence. This was also the age of Socrates, Plato's teacher, whose method of relentless questioning to expose contradictions became a foundation of Western philosophy and critical thinking.

Modern successor: the very word "democracy" (demos + kratos, "people power") comes from this system, and modern representative democracies, jury trials, and even some proposals for lottery-based civic panels all trace back to Athenian practice. The Socratic method remains a core teaching technique in law schools and philosophy departments today.

**Recovered Artifact (on Ideal path):** 🏛️ Assembly Procedure Scroll — A papyrus scroll detailing Athenian democratic process.

## 5. 🏺 The Aqueduct Blueprint Heist
**Location:** Rome, Roman Republic  |  **Era:** c. 100 BCE

### Briefing
You land near the office of a Roman censor, one of the officials responsible for public contracts and civic works, just as news breaks that engineering plans for a new aqueduct arch have vanished overnight. In their place: a strange lightweight tube of a material no artisan in Rome has ever seen. The censor's clerks are frantic — without the plans, the aqueduct's next arch cannot be built to the precise gradient Roman engineers require.

Down the street, the Forum bustles with senators, contractors, and clerks haggling over public works, since Roman law required contracts for aqueducts, roads, and sewers to be recorded and open to scrutiny. Beneath the city, the Cloaca Maxima — Rome's great sewer — channels waste out toward the river, its tunnels known mainly to the laborers who maintain them. Beyond the city gates, milestones mark every road with exact distances, a system so precise that couriers use it to time their journeys.

Somewhere between the Forum's contract records, the sewer tunnels, and the road network's obsessive precision, the Agency believes the plans — and whoever took them — can still be found.

### Decision Tree

**Opening question:** Your first move investigating the missing aqueduct blueprint?
> *Stakes hint: Rome runs on precision — the sharpest lead uses that precision against the thief.*

- **A.** Study the aqueduct's gradient calculations to see who had the surveying skill to use the plans
- **B.** Question laborers at the construction site, since they're on-site every day and notice when something's off
- **C.** Search the Forum for suspicious activity around the public contract records

#### Path A: "Study the aqueduct's gradient calculations to see who had the surveying skill to use the plans"

**Follow-up question:** The gradient calculations require rare surveying expertise. You learn a rival engineer was recently passed over for this very contract. How do you proceed?

- **AA.** Check the Senate's public contract records for his recent bid history on public works
  - **Outcome (✅ IDEAL, +30 pts):** The Senate's contract records confirm your suspicion — the rival engineer bid on this exact project and lost it by a narrow margin. Following his known movements to the Cloaca Maxima, Rome's obsessively precise milestones let you predict his escape route to the foot. You catch him as he emerges, blueprint in hand, and the aqueduct project continues on schedule.
- **AB.** Confront him with no evidence, betting that guilt alone will make him crack under pressure
  - **Outcome (🔴 CATASTROPHIC (Case Mishandled), -10 pts):** Confronting him with no evidence backfires — offended and unwilling to cooperate, he alerts contacts across the Forum that an agent is asking pointless questions. Whatever trail existed goes quiet fast.
- **AC.** Dismiss him as too obvious a suspect, since the real thief would surely be harder to guess
  - **Outcome (⚪ NON-IDEAL (Trail Went Cold), +0 pts):** Dismissing the obvious suspect turns out to be a mistake; he was, in fact, exactly who you should have investigated. The blueprints are never recovered.

### The Real History
The Meiji Restoration (1868) ended over two centuries of feudal Tokugawa shogunate rule and rapidly transformed Japan into a modern industrial and military power in just decades. The new government abolished the samurai class's special privileges, built railways and telegraph lines, sent students and officials abroad to study Western technology and institutions, and adopted a new constitution — all while deliberately preserving distinctly Japanese cultural identity rather than simply copying the West.

Modern successor: this era's blend of rapid technological adoption with cultural continuity is often cited as a model for national modernization, and directly set the foundation for Japan's 20th-century emergence as an industrial and technological power — the same drive visible in modern Japanese manufacturing, robotics, and rail engineering (including the Shinkansen "bullet train" lineage).
